OVERVIEW

As part of O'Reilly Auto Parts' modernization initiative, our UX team began exploring what the future of the company's retail applications could become.

The first effort focused on reimagining the Point of Sale experience, but as our work progressed, we realized we were trying to solve two different problems at the same time. One project focused on designing the future Point of Sale experience. The other focused on creating the design language that would support that application, along with future products across the company.

Splitting these efforts gave each project a clear purpose. The Future Point of Sale became a product discovery initiative, while Drift became the design system that would establish a shared foundation for future applications.

Project Highlights

  • Created a shared design language for enterprise applications
  • Established principles, documentation, and reusable components
  • Collaborated with design, product, and engineering partners
  • Selected as O'Reilly Auto Parts' future company-wide design system
THE PROBLEM

The original modernization effort exposed two challenges. The first was scope. We had combined a product redesign and a design system into one initiative, making it difficult for either project to move forward effectively. The second was consistency. Different products across the company had evolved independently over time, each with its own design language, interaction patterns, and documentation. As new applications were planned, there wasn't a shared foundation that design and engineering teams could build from.

To understand the current landscape, I completed a UI inventory across existing retail applications and documented the different visual styles and interaction patterns already in use. I also benchmarked competing retail products to identify common patterns and opportunities that could influence our direction.

These findings reinforced the need for a design system that could support current work while scaling to future products.

THE APPROACH

Creating a foundation before building components

Once the projects were separated, our focus shifted toward defining what Drift should become. Before creating components, our team worked together to establish the guiding principles that would shape the design system and provide a shared philosophy for future decisions. Those conversations eventually became the Drift Design System Principles & Philosophy.

Our Purpose

Our unified design system exists to create cohesive, accessible, and scalable user experiences across all platforms — web and mobile — regardless of codebase or team. It is a single source of truth for design and development, enabling faster delivery and better usability.

Our Promise

By unifying our design systems

  • We ship faster, with greater confidence.
  • We provide delightful, inclusive, and consistent experiences.
  • We create a system that serves our users not just our teams.

As the project gained momentum, I helped create the name Drift and designed the wordmark for the system. Giving the design system its own identity made it easier to talk about across the organization and helped build visibility with product teams and stakeholders.

Creating a shared language

As our conversations became more detailed, we needed a way to explain how the design system was organized. I adapted Brad Frost's Atomic Design framework into what became Auto-mic Design, a taxonomy built around automotive concepts that felt natural within O'Reilly. Instead of Atoms, Molecules, and Organisms, the system was organized as:

  • Nuts & Bolts
  • Parts
  • Assemblies
  • Car Systems
  • Blueprints
  • Whole Car

The framework gave designers, engineers, product managers, and business stakeholders a common vocabulary for discussing everything from design tokens to complete application experiences. It also connected the design system to the company's identity instead of relying on terminology borrowed from another industry.

Building the system

With the foundation established, the team shifted into building the design system itself. We divided component creation across the three designers, regularly reviewed one another's work, and documented decisions as the library continued to evolve.

Alongside the component work, I wrote several pieces of the supporting documentation, including the Drift Principles & Philosophy, Voice & Tone guidelines, Accessibility guidelines, and Taxonomy. These documents helped establish consistent guidance for teams beyond the visual components themselves.

As Drift matured, we also created governance documentation that defined how future components would be proposed, reviewed, and added to the system.

Around the same time, I began collaborating with engineering to understand technical considerations and implementation strategies so both teams could align early in the process.

Google Material Design served as a starting point for many interaction patterns, but we adapted those patterns to fit our users, products, and business needs rather than adopting the system as-is.

OUTCOME

As Drift continued to mature, the company hired a third-party consultancy to create a design system for other areas of the business. At the time, we assumed our work would eventually become part of whatever solution they delivered. Rather than slowing down, we continued building Drift with the expectation that our work could be adapted if needed.

The opposite happened. Leadership saw greater value in the direction our internal team had established than in the work delivered by the consultants. The consulting engagement ended, and Drift was selected as the design system that will serve as the shared foundation across the company.

The long-term vision is for Drift to provide the core design language while allowing individual products and departments to extend the system for their own needs without losing consistency across the organization. Today the project continues to evolve.

  • The guiding principles and documentation are established.
  • The component library is approximately 90% complete.
  • Design tokens and variables are ready for engineering implementation.
  • Engineering has begun planning a headless component architecture.
  • Drift is serving as the design foundation for the Future Point of Sale and future modernization efforts.
REFLECTION

This project expanded my understanding of what it takes to create a design system that can scale across an organization. Building reusable components was an important part of the work, but it depended on having shared principles, common terminology, and clear documentation that designers, engineers, and product teams could all understand and reference.

Separating Drift from the Future Point of Sale also changed the trajectory of both projects. Once each initiative had its own focus, the team was able to make progress more deliberately while still supporting the same modernization effort.

Looking back, I'm especially proud of the work that helped shape the system's identity. Naming Drift, creating the Auto-mic Design taxonomy, and writing foundational guidance gave the project a common language that teams could rally around as the system continues to grow.
