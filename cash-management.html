PROBLEM

Workflows that drifted from daily reality

Store opening and closing procedures are some of the most repetitive and time-sensitive workflows employees perform. The Cash Management flow had gradually accumulated unnecessary complexity over time, creating friction for employees trying to complete these tasks quickly and accurately.

Through heuristic evaluation and user interviews, I discovered several recurring issues:

01

Employees routinely skipped screens because they didn't provide value.

02

Some forms allowed the same information to be entered multiple ways, creating unnecessary confusion.

03

One screen presented three separate tables as one large block of information with little visual hierarchy.

04

Two consecutive screens contained nearly identical information, forcing users through an unnecessary extra step.

05

Modal dialogs throughout the experience had become inconsistent and no longer followed our design standards.

06

Labels and terminology often reflected internal business language rather than how employees described their work.

07

Several layouts prioritized displaying information instead of helping employees complete tasks efficiently.

Rather than isolated UI problems, these were symptoms of workflows that had slowly drifted away from the reality of daily store operations.

CONSTRAINTS

When usability meets the business

Although many usability improvements appeared straightforward, several business constraints shaped the final solution.

One proposed improvement was combining two nearly identical screens into one. I believed the screens could be safely consolidated after observing employee behavior. However, Store Operations initially objected due to legal and loss-prevention concerns.

Instead of accepting the constraint at face value, we brought together Store Operations, Legal, and Loss Prevention to review both the user research and the proposed redesign. After understanding how employees actually completed the workflow, those stakeholders agreed the screens could be combined while still satisfying operational requirements.

Not every proposed improvement survived. One interaction I redesigned was later challenged after stakeholders introduced an operational step I had not previously understood. With a better understanding of the full workflow, I reversed that decision and redesigned the experience around the newly uncovered requirement.

“

Good design isn't about defending ideas, it's about improving understanding to work toward the best solution.

APPROACH & DESIGN DECISIONS

Partnering, observing, and pressure-testing

I partnered closely with our Product Manager throughout the project. Together we combined several research methods:

01
Heuristic evaluation
02
Employee usability sessions
03
Observation of real workflows
04
Stakeholder collaboration

My research began with regular store visits, not to evaluate specific features, but to understand the environment employees worked in. This helped me build empathy for their work and establish trust before conducting project-specific research. As a result, employees were more comfortable sharing honest feedback and thinking aloud as they completed real tasks.

Rather than simply asking employees what they wanted, I asked them to perform their normal tasks while thinking aloud. This revealed where users hesitated, where they became confused, and where they openly admitted:

"We usually skip this."

Those moments became some of the project's most valuable insights.

From findings to redesign

Workflow simplification

  • Consolidated two nearly identical screens into one after validating business constraints.
  • Removed unnecessary steps employees consistently bypassed.
  • Simplified information entry where duplicate methods created confusion.
BEFORE: TWO SCREENS
AFTER: ONE SCREEN

Information architecture

  • Introduced clearer hierarchy where dense tables previously appeared as one continuous block.
  • Moved secondary information behind contextual tooltips to reduce visual noise.
  • Improved scanability throughout the experience.

UI consistency

  • Updated numerous legacy modal dialogs to match current design standards.
  • Improved consistency across components and interaction patterns.

Matching software to real workflows

  • Reordered input fields to match the printed paper form employees complete, reducing mental effort between the digital and physical tasks.
  • Updated terminology to reflect how employees describe their work, not internal corporate language.

Operational improvements

  • Proposed collapsing sections of printed reports whenever values were zero or unused, making documentation shorter and easier to review.
  • Even digitally, it's better to show only what is relevant.
OUTCOME

A shorter, clearer, better-aligned workflow

The redesign produced improvements across the entire workflow rather than solving a single isolated issue. The experience became:

REFLECTIONS

Challenge assumptions, including your own

This project reinforced two connected lessons.

01

Research uncovers realities stakeholders never see

Business processes are usually designed around intended workflows, but employees adapt those workflows over time. Understanding those adaptations is essential before redesigning the experience.

02

Research reveals gaps in our own understanding

Several redesign ideas that initially seemed obvious became less appropriate after additional stakeholder conversations exposed operational requirements I hadn't yet uncovered.

Good design comes from understanding the system before trying to improve it. Sometimes that means validating existing practices. Sometimes it means changing long-standing processes. Either way, the goal isn't proving that design is right, the goal is helping everyone better understand the problem so the team can make better decisions together.
