PROBLEM

A checkout app that grew into everything

Over decades of growth, the point of sale had gradually expanded far beyond its original purpose. What began as a checkout application had evolved into an all-in-one tool containing inventory functions, operational workflows, reporting, backroom tools, manager utilities, and countless other features.

The result was a product that attempted to serve every need at every moment. Employees were presented with an overwhelming number of options regardless of the task they were performing, increasing cognitive load and making common checkout tasks more complex than necessary.

Many of these interaction patterns also reflected decisions inherited from legacy green-screen software. Although the interface had been modernized visually, much of the underlying navigation, information architecture, and workflow thinking remained rooted in decades-old constraints.

The business recognized the need to modernize the point of sale. Our design team believed the opportunity was much larger than a visual refresh.

CONSTRAINTS

Balancing vision with validation

This project represented the first phase of a much larger modernization effort. The business was eager to explore future concepts and visual direction, while the design team believed meaningful redesign required deeper research before committing to interface solutions.

That created an important balance: we needed to demonstrate strategic possibilities while also advocating for additional discovery before large design investments were made. Because the existing platform supported thousands of stores and years of established workflows, any future solution would also need to consider adoption, employee familiarity, technical feasibility, and incremental rollout.

Rather than attempting a complete replacement, the goal became defining a long-term vision that could realistically evolve over time.

APPROACH & DESIGN DECISIONS

From feature sprawl to a focused ecosystem

Working as one of three UX designers, I helped establish the strategic direction for what a future point of sale could become.

Competitive research

I researched point of sale systems across multiple retailers to identify common interaction patterns, opportunities, and emerging trends. Rather than looking for ideas to copy, I looked for patterns worth understanding and principles that could be adapted to our environment.

Collaborative ideation

Our team independently explored blue-sky concepts before comparing ideas. The concepts that consistently emerged across multiple designers became areas for deeper exploration:

  • Global navigation
  • Persistent contextual side panels
  • Smarter search
  • Simplified workflows
  • Clearer information architecture

Rethinking the product architecture

One of my largest contributions was mapping the entire point of sale ecosystem and evaluating what truly belonged within checkout. I created a site map of the existing product and proposed separating customer-facing checkout workflows from operational and backroom tools, rather than one increasingly bloated application, two focused products:

Point of Sale

A streamlined product dedicated to customer transactions.

Operations Toolbox

Inventory management, reports, annual inventory, distribution center returns, and other operational tools.

This approach reduced complexity while creating a stronger foundation for future scalability and mobile use.

Site map: proposed product split

Information architecture & workflows

I mapped workflows by customer journey rather than by feature, from the moment a customer approached the counter through payment, returns, and post-transaction tasks, clarifying which tools truly belonged inside the checkout moment.

Design system foundations

Alongside strategy, our team began laying groundwork for a shared design system so future teams could build on a consistent visual and interaction language.

OUTCOME

REFLECTIONS

Defining the right problem first

Designers naturally want to create solutions. Sometimes the most valuable contribution is defining the right problem first. Although stakeholders initially requested future concepts, our team consistently advocated for additional research before committing to major design decisions.

That tension highlighted an important responsibility of senior designers: our role isn't simply producing screens, it's helping organizations decide what should be designed in the first place.

The project also deepened my appreciation for systems thinking. The interface was never the primary challenge. The real opportunity was understanding how architecture, workflows, information, employee behavior, technology, and business processes all worked together. Modernizing the product meant modernizing the thinking behind it.

If given the opportunity to continue the work, my next step would not be higher-fidelity designs. It would be validating every major assumption through field research before moving into iterative prototyping and incremental implementation.
